Lecture notes: loss and gradient descent

Sample lecture notes written for Adle's tests. Page one of three.

Measuring error with a loss function

A neural network makes a prediction, and we need a single number that says how wrong the prediction was. That number is the loss. For a regression problem the usual choice is the mean squared error: subtract the target from the prediction, square the difference, and average over all of the training examples.

Squaring does two jobs. It makes every error positive, so that errors in opposite directions cannot cancel out, and it punishes large mistakes much more than small ones.

The gradient points uphill

Page two of three.

Imagine the loss as the height of a landscape whose coordinates are the weights of the network. The gradient at a point is the direction in which the ground rises most steeply. To reduce the loss we therefore step in the opposite direction.

The chain rule lets us compute this direction for every weight, even in a deep network, by multiplying together the local rates of change along the path from the weight to the loss. Backpropagation is the bookkeeping that makes this efficient.

Choosing the learning rate

Page three of three.

The learning rate decides how far each step goes. A very small learning rate is safe but slow. A very large learning rate overshoots the valley and can make the loss increase instead of decrease. In practice the learning rate is tuned by trying several values and watching how the loss behaves over time.

Worked example: with a weight of 2.0, a gradient of 0.5, and a learning rate of 0.1, the updated weight is 2.0 minus 0.1 times 0.5, which equals 1.95.
